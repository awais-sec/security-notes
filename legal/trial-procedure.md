# Trial Procedure: Charge, Plea, Evidence, Acquittal and Conviction

## I. The Charge

- **What is a charge?** An accusation against someone for allegedly committing a crime.
- **Why have a charge?** To clearly tell the accused what they're accused of, so they can prepare a defense.
- **Separate charges**: Generally each offense gets its own charge, with exceptions:
  - Up to three similar offenses in 12 months can be in one charge.
  - Connected offenses in the same incident can be in one charge.
  - If it's unclear which offense occurred, all can be included.
  - Multiple people can be charged jointly under specific conditions.
- **What a charge must include**: The specific offense, its legal name (if it has one), and enough detail about the offense.
- **Changing the charge**: The court can change it anytime before final judgment.

## II. The Plea

- **Pleading guilty**: Must be recorded word-for-word in question-and-answer format. If the accused admits guilt without doubt, the court can convict.
- **Pleading not guilty**: The trial begins.
- **Remaining silent**: Not an admission of guilt, it's the accused's right; the trial proceeds as a precautionary measure.
- **Changing a plea**: A second plea on the same charge can only be recorded when the charge is amended, otherwise courts aren't empowered to record another plea.
- **Withdrawing charges**: Possible with the court's permission, especially with multiple charges.

## III. Evidence

- **What is evidence?** Statements from witnesses, parties, and documents that prove or disprove the case.
- **Types**: Oral (witness testimony), documentary (documents presented), primary (original document), secondary (copies, certified copies, mechanically produced copies).
- **Who can testify?** Anyone, unless the court believes they can't understand questions, are too young/old/sick, or have been convicted of perjury without repenting.
- **Deaf or dumb witnesses**: Can still testify through writing or signs.
- **Order of evidence**: Prosecution presents first, then the accused is examined, then the defense presents last.
- **How evidence is given**: Examination-in-chief (prosecution), cross-examination (defense), then re-examination (prosecution).
- **Relevance and admissibility**: Relevant facts help the court decide the case; admissible facts are allowed in court as long as they're reliable. The judge decides what's allowed.
- **Burden of proof**: The prosecution must prove guilt; the accused only needs to create reasonable doubt.
- **Quality over quantity**: The court cares more about evidence quality than the number of witnesses.
- **Character evidence**: Good character is relevant for the accused; bad character is generally irrelevant.

## IV. Acquittal and Conviction

- **Acquittal at any stage**: A magistrate can acquit if there's no chance of conviction.
- **What is acquittal?** A formal declaration of innocence, given when the accused is found not guilty after hearing evidence and examination.
- **Double jeopardy**: A person can't be tried or punished for the same offense twice.
- **What is conviction?** A finding of guilt.
- **Sentencing**: The punishment given after conviction, which should match the crime.
- **Mitigating circumstances**: Can lessen punishment but don't excuse the crime.
- **Factors for punishment**: Nature of the offense, circumstances, deliberation, provocation, history of the accused, age, and character.
- **Principles of sentencing**: Retribution, deterrence, prevention, rehabilitation.

## V. Other Important Points

- **Questioning the accused**: The court can ask questions to clarify evidence.
- **Final arguments**: Both sides give closing statements after all evidence is presented.
- **Judgment**: The court's decision, including the facts, the decision itself, reasons, the final order (conviction or acquittal), and the sentence if applicable.
