# Domain 1: Information System Auditing Process

## Business Processes in IS Auditing

IS auditors need to understand and evaluate business processes, test operational controls, and identify different types of controls (policies, procedures, practices, and organizational structures).

**IS internal audit function** should be established by an audit charter approved by the board of directors or senior management.

**Audit planning** involves listing processes, performing risk assessments, and constructing an audit plan that prioritizes high-risk processes.

**Laws and regulations** shape IS audit functions directly. Auditors need to understand relevant regulations to determine whether an organization is actually compliant.

### Audit vs. audit charter vs. engagement letter

- **Audit**: A formal examination and/or testing of information systems to assess compliance with applicable laws, regulations, contracts, and industry guidelines. Also evaluates whether systems and processes comply with governance criteria and relevant policies, and whether they maintain data confidentiality, integrity, availability, and operational efficiency.
- **Audit charter**: An overarching document approved by the board or senior management, defining the purpose, authority, and responsibility of the internal audit function across the entire entity.
- **Engagement letter**: More narrowly scoped, it outlines the auditor's responsibilities for one specific audit exercise with a particular objective.

## Risk and Risk-Based Auditing

Risk-based auditing means planning and executing audits with a focus on the highest-risk areas, to prioritize resources and effort accordingly.

### Types of risk

- **Inherent risk**: The susceptibility of a process or entity to significant problems, without considering any controls in place.
- **Control risk**: The possibility that an error won't be prevented or detected by internal controls.
- **Detection risk**: The chance that an auditor fails to catch a material misstatement or error.

### Risk response options

- **Mitigation**: Implement controls to reduce the risk.
- **Acceptance**: Choose not to act, because the risk falls within the organization's acceptance criteria.
- **Avoidance**: Don't engage in the activity that introduces the risk.
- **Transfer/sharing**: Shift the risk to another party.

### Risk-based audit steps

1. **Planning**: Identify audit areas, assess risk, develop the audit plan.
2. **Execution**: Carry out audit procedures based on the plan.
3. **Reporting**: Communicate findings and recommendations.

## Sampling

Sampling means analyzing a representative subset of data or transactions when examining the entire population isn't practical.

- **Statistical sampling**: Uses mathematical principles to determine sample size and selection, allowing quantification of sampling risk.
- **Non-statistical sampling**: Relies on auditor judgment instead of mathematical technique.

### Sampling methods

- **Attribute sampling**: Used in compliance testing to determine whether a certain attribute is present or absent within a population.
- **Stop-or-go sampling**: Minimizes sample size and audit work by allowing an early stop once a condition is met.
- **Discovery sampling**: Designed to find at least one instance of a significant error or fraud.
- **Stratified mean sampling**: Divides the population into subgroups (strata) and applies mean-per-unit estimation within each for more precise results.

## Testing Methods

- **Compliance testing**: Verifies that controls are actually operating effectively during the audit period. Focused on control design.
- **Substantive testing**: Gathers evidence on the completeness, accuracy, or existence of actual activities/transactions during the audit period.

## Quality Assurance and Audit Process Improvement

- **Control Self-Assessment (CSA)**: A management technique that shifts some control-monitoring responsibility to functional areas, enhancing (not replacing) the internal audit function.
- **Integrated auditing**: Identifies risks and key controls, and tests that management controls actually operate effectively, resulting in a comprehensive report on control risk, design, and weaknesses.
- **Audit documentation**: Provides evidence supporting audit findings and conclusions. Needs to be clear, complete, and easily retrievable, and include records of planning, execution, and findings.
- **Follow-up activities**: Auditors confirm that management has taken appropriate corrective action, managed through a formal follow-up program and communicated back to management.

### Quality Management vs. Quality Assurance vs. Quality Control

- **Quality Management**: Overseeing all activities and tasks to maintain a desired level of excellence, including creating a quality policy and implementing quality planning, assurance, improvement, and control.
- **Quality Assurance**: Part of quality management focused on providing confidence that quality requirements will be met, through systematic activities within the quality system.
- **Quality Control**: A system of maintaining standards by testing a sample of output against specification, using operational techniques to fulfill quality requirements.

## Key Definitions: Risk, Threat, Vulnerability

**Risk** is the combination of the probability of an event and its consequence:

`Risk = Likelihood x Impact`

IT risk specifically is the business risk associated with the use, ownership, operation, involvement, influence, and adoption of IT within an enterprise.

Risk is also framed as the intersection of assets, threats, and vulnerabilities, the potential for loss, damage, or destruction of an asset as a result of a threat exploiting a vulnerability:

`Risk = Threat x Vulnerability`

**Threat**: A statement of intent to harm or punish, or something that presents imminent danger or harm. Example: a person with the potential to blow up a building is a threat.

**Vulnerability**: A weakness that can be exploited by a threat actor (such as an attacker) to perform unauthorized actions within a system, or a gap in a security program that can be exploited by threats to gain unauthorized access to an asset.

Information security risk, taken together, comprises the impacts to an organization and its stakeholders that could occur due to the threats and vulnerabilities associated with the operation and use of information systems and the environments those systems operate in.
