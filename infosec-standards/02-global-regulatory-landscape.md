# Global Regulatory Landscape: Policies, Standards, GRC, and Regional Frameworks

## 1. Policies, Standards, and Regulations

Organizations must navigate three distinct levels of guidance to ensure security:

- **Policies** — high-level statements created by an organization to define its internal rules, principles, and security direction; guide employees and management on protecting information (e.g. a company password policy)
- **Standards** — guidelines and best practices (like ISO/IEC 27001, NIST, or PCI DSS) that provide a framework for implementing strong cybersecurity controls; ensure consistency across organizations worldwide and help build trust and reputation through certification
- **Regulations** — laws or rules set by governments or regulatory bodies that organizations are legally required to follow, aimed at protecting citizens, industries, and nations from cyber risks

## 2. Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC)

The GRC framework helps an organization align its IT operations with its business objectives:

- **Governance** — the way an organization is directed and controlled; leadership sets policies, defines responsibilities, and ensures security aligns with business goals (e.g. a board appointing a CISO)
- **Risk** — the process of identifying, assessing, and managing threats that could harm assets or reputation; organizations use risk assessments to prioritize security measures, such as enforcing MFA if weak passwords are identified as a threat
- **Compliance** — ensuring the organization follows all relevant laws and standards, helping avoid legal penalties and build customer trust (e.g. a Pakistani healthcare startup following PECA and HIPAA)

## 3. Regional and Industry-Specific Frameworks (Overview)

- **PECA (Prevention of Electronic Crimes Act, 2016)** — a Pakistani law that defines electronic crimes and provides the legal power to investigate and punish offenders, enforced by the FIA (Federal Investigation Agency) Cyber Crime Wing (full detail in section 5 below)
- **PCI DSS** — a global security standard designed specifically to protect credit and debit card payment data and prevent fraud during transactions
- **COBIT** — a framework focused on IT governance and management, ensuring IT investments provide business value
- **SOC 2** — an auditing standard for service providers (especially cloud companies) that evaluates systems based on five principles: Security, Availability, Processing Integrity, Confidentiality, and Privacy

## 4. International Cooperation: The Budapest Convention

Adopted in 2001 by the Council of Europe, the Budapest Convention on Cybercrime was the first international treaty designed to address internet crimes by harmonizing national laws and boosting international investigative cooperation.

- **Scope** — specifically deals with offenses against the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of computer data, such as hacking, wiretapping, data interference (deleting data), system interference (DDoS attacks), and the misuse of hacking tools
- **Primary Objectives** — developing efficient investigation methods, improving international cooperation, and protecting human rights during digital forensics
- **Membership Status** — while many non-European nations like the USA, Japan, and Australia have joined, several major countries — including Pakistan, India, China, and Russia — are not members
- **Common Criticisms** — some nations argue the convention gives foreign agencies too much power over domestic systems, or that it primarily reflects Western interests. Critics also note that technology evolves faster than the treaty, leaving new threats like AI-powered attacks not fully covered

**Case Study — International Cooperation:** the Yahoo Data Breach (2013–2014) stands as one of the most famous real-world applications of the Budapest Convention's principles. The investigation required extensive international collaboration to track the perpetrators and manage the global impact of the breach.

## 5. Deep Dive: Pakistan's PECA Law

The Prevention of Electronic Crimes Act (PECA), 2016, is the primary federal law in Pakistan designed to define electronic crimes and provide a legal framework for the investigation and punishment of offenders. It is officially enforced by the Federal Investigation Agency (FIA) Cyber Crime Wing.

**Core Objectives:** the law aims to prevent cybercrimes such as hacking, identity theft, and online harassment, while protecting the government, organizations, and individuals from digital threats.

**Key Offenses Covered:**
- Unauthorized access to systems (hacking) and spreading malicious code (viruses)
- Cyber terrorism and attacks on critical national infrastructure
- Electronic fraud, forgery, and identity theft
- Cyberstalking and online harassment, with specific focus on protecting women
- Distribution of child pornography and other illegal content
- Spreading hate speech or false information on social media platforms

**Limitations and Criticisms of PECA:**
- **Freedom of Speech** — significant concerns that the law has been misused to target journalists, activists, and critics, potentially suppressing freedom of speech
- **Technical Gaps** — the FIA often lacks the advanced technical expertise and resources required to investigate highly sophisticated cybercrimes
- **Jurisdictional Issues** — PECA struggles to address cybercriminals operating outside of Pakistan's borders due to the cross-border nature of digital crime
- **Awareness** — a large portion of the Pakistani population remains unaware of their legal rights and protections under this act

## 6. Case Study: Pakistani Payments Company

This scenario highlights the real-world consequences of failing to align growth with INFOSEC standards.

- **The Situation** — a mid-sized Pakistani payments company expanded rapidly into the EU and Middle East but failed to update its security to meet international standards (ISO/IEC 27001, PCI DSS) or national regulations (PECA, State Bank of Pakistan guidelines)
- **The Incident** — a data exposure occurred, affecting both EU and Pakistani citizens
- **Business Impact:**
  - Loss of Trust — clients questioned the company's maturity
  - Regulatory Risk — the breach of EU data triggered strict GDPR obligations and potential fines
  - Financial Loss — the company faced high costs for incident investigation, emergency audits, and service disruptions, leading to delays in new contracts
- **Remediation** — the company had to develop a roadmap to fix governance gaps and balance its conflicting international and federal legal obligations
