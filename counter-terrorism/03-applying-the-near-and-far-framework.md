## Scenario 1: The FLQ Kidnapping (Quebec, 1970)

A militant group called the FLQ wanted independence for Quebec. Their original plan was to kidnap an American diplomat. However, police had set up roadblocks. The group changed their plan at the last minute. They drove to the home of Pierre Laporte, the Vice-Premier of Quebec, who lived in their neighborhood. They kidnapped him from his front lawn while he played football with his nephew.

**Question 1:** Which concept from the lecture does this scenario best illustrate? Choose one: (a) routine terrorism, (b) journey to crime, or (c) the Crowe Report. Explain your answer in one sentence.

**Answer:** This scenario best illustrates (b) journey to crime because the group abandoned their original far target and chose a victim who was geographically closer and more accessible after encountering police roadblocks.

**Question 2:** Was this a "near" attack or a "far" attack for the FLQ? Explain why.

**Answer:** This was a "near" attack because the FLQ kidnapped Pierre Laporte from his home in their own neighborhood, which was close to their base of operations, rather than traveling to a distant target like an American diplomat.

## Scenario 2: Timothy McVeigh and the Oklahoma City Bombing (1995)

Timothy McVeigh wanted to attack a federal building. He considered targets in Washington D.C., Kansas City, and Little Rock. He spent 13 months traveling through Colorado, Texas, Kansas, and Oklahoma. He rented storage units in Kansas and bought bomb materials from multiple stores. On December 18, 1994, he chose the Murrah Building in Oklahoma City. He rejected Washington D.C. because driving a truck full of explosives to the capital was too difficult and risky.

**Question 3:** McVeigh traveled through four states. Does this contradict the "journey to crime" principle that offenders choose targets close to home? Why or why not? (Hint: compare McVeigh's behavior to the FLQ case.)

**Answer:** No, this does not contradict the journey to crime principle because McVeigh ultimately chose Oklahoma City, which was relatively closer to his known locations and operational base, whereas the FLQ case involved an immediate shift to a nearby target when faced with obstacles, while McVeigh systematically eliminated far targets like Washington D.C. due to risk and difficulty.

**Question 4:** According to the lecture, what is the main reason McVeigh rejected Washington D.C. as a target? Use the concept of "proximity" in your answer.

**Answer:** The main reason McVeigh rejected Washington D.C. was lack of proximity, meaning the distance was too great, and driving a truck full of explosives to the capital involved too many risks, security checkpoints, and opportunities for detection along the way.

## Scenario 3: The 1996 Manchester Bombing

The Provisional Irish Republican Army (IRA) was based in Northern Ireland. In 1996, they detonated a 3,300-pound bomb in Manchester, England. This was the largest bomb in Great Britain since World War II. The IRA gave a warning call before the blast, which allowed police to evacuate 75,000 to 80,000 people. No one was killed, but over 200 people were injured, and the bomb caused £700 million in damage.

**Question 5:** Was the Manchester bombing a "routine" attack or a "far/spectacular" attack? Use the IRA data from the lecture (14,415 incidents in Northern Ireland vs. 1 in England in 1972) to support your answer.

**Answer:** The Manchester bombing was a "far/spectacular" attack because the vast majority of IRA attacks (14,415 incidents) occurred in Northern Ireland close to their base, while attacks in England were extremely rare, with only 1 recorded in 1972, making the 1996 Manchester bombing an exception that required long-distance travel and significant planning.

**Question 6:** The IRA gave a warning call before the bomb exploded. According to the lecture, why did the IRA do this? (Hint: think about public opinion and the five IRA adaptations.)

**Answer:** The IRA gave a warning call to avoid mass civilian casualties, which helped them maintain public opinion and political legitimacy, and it was one of their five adaptations to reduce backlash while still causing economic damage and disruption.

## Scenario 4: The US Invasion of Iraq (2003)

After the September 11 attacks, President Bush announced a policy of "taking it to them." The United States invaded Iraq in 2003. The goal was to fight terrorists on their soil so they could not attack the American homeland. After the invasion, American soldiers, bases, and convoys became widespread throughout Iraq. From April 2003 through December 2005, Iraq saw a consistent and rising pattern of terrorist attacks.

**Question 7:** According to Clarke and Newman's analysis (Slide 15), did the invasion of Iraq prevent attacks on the US homeland? What did it actually create?

**Answer:** According to Clarke and Newman's analysis, the invasion of Iraq did not prevent attacks on the US homeland; instead, it actually created a new frontline for terrorism and transformed Iraq into a training ground and operational hub for terrorists who had previously not focused on American targets.

**Question 8:** Before 2003, terrorists based in Iraq had almost no American targets to attack. After 2003, American targets were everywhere. Which concept from the lecture does this illustrate: (a) the Inman Report, (b) routine terrorism, or (c) the Ring of Steel? Explain why.

**Answer:** This illustrates (b) routine terrorism because the presence of American soldiers, bases, and convoys created routine, predictable targets that terrorists could attack regularly, turning Iraq into a high-frequency terrorism environment.
