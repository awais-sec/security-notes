# Document Examination and Investigative Interview Techniques

Exam-prep notes split into objective (MCQ-style) topics and subjective (essay-style) topics, covering forensic document examination and investigative interviewing.

## Objective Topics

### 1. Indented Writing

- **Definition**: the impression a writing instrument leaves on the sheets of paper below the one that contains the original writing.
- **Significance in fraud cases**: fraud examiners should watch for incriminating indented writings, or the absence of expected indentations, both of which can be valuable in proving fraud.
- **Proof of alteration**: in documents routinely kept in multi-page sets (legal forms, invoices, notebooks), writing on one sheet should correspond to indentations on the following sheet. Missing or mismatched indentations can prove pages were removed or substituted.
- **What not to do**: never try to develop indented writing by shading or scratching the surface with a pencil. This reveals only deep indentations, misses faint ones, and can prevent further expert examination.
- **Methods of detection**:
  - **Oblique-lighting method**: take the document into subdued lighting, place it on a flat surface, and shine a bright, narrow beam low and parallel to the page. Move the light around; shadows in the indentations only show at right angles to the beam.
  - **Electrostatic Detection Apparatus (ESDA)**: a specialized technique using lighting, photographic methods, and sensitive instruments to detect and permanently record extremely faint indentations the oblique-lighting method can't pick up.

### 2. Inhibitors of Communication

An inhibitor is any social-psychological barrier that keeps a respondent unable or unwilling to give relevant information. The interviewer's job is to minimize inhibitors and maximize facilitators.

**Four inhibitors that make a respondent unwilling:**

1. **Competing demands for time**: the respondent hesitates because of other priorities; the interviewer needs to establish the interview is worth their time.
2. **Ego threat**: withholding information due to a perceived threat to self-esteem.
   - *Repression*: the strongest form; a defense mechanism keeping anxiety-causing memories unconscious (e.g., an embezzler genuinely "forgetting" an act that violates their own moral code).
   - *Disapproval*: hesitating to admit something for fear of the interviewer's judgment; an accepting, sympathetic attitude helps.
   - *Loss of status*: fear that public knowledge of the information would cost them standing; assurances of confidentiality can help.
3. **Etiquette**: the answer feels inappropriate or in poor taste to share; choosing the right interviewer and setting helps.
4. **Trauma**: an acutely unpleasant feeling tied to crisis experiences, resurfacing when reporting them. Common with victims; sensitive handling helps.

**Four inhibitors that make a respondent unable to give information:**

1. **Forgetting**: memory fades over time, and the ego-defense system can reconstruct the past by omission, addition, or distortion.
2. **Chronological confusion**: confusing the order of events, common when reconstructing a case history.
3. **Inferential confusion**: errors arising from *induction* (converting concrete experiences into generalizations) or *deduction* (giving concrete examples for a category).
4. **Unconscious behaviour**: revealed through custom/habit, circular reaction (unwitting response to another's nonverbal cues), or response to an acute emotional crisis.

### 3. Types of Fingerprints

- **Latent (invisible) fingerprints**: unique identifiers left by body oils, salts, and amino acids from friction ridges on fingers, palms, and soles.
  - **Durability**: can survive decades on protected porous surfaces (paper); deteriorate quickly on hard, nonporous surfaces if handled or exposed to heat, humidity, or water.
  - **Handling documents**: wear gloves to avoid smudging; if bare-handed contact by known people occurred, note their names for elimination.
  - **On porous surfaces (paper)**: never dust with powder. Preserve the document in a labeled protective container instead; experts use iodine fuming, silver nitrate, or ninhydrin spray.
  - **On hard, nonporous surfaces**: can be dusted with fingerprint powder or photocopier toner and lifted with tape onto an index card. Preferably retain the item and submit it to an expert, since some prints won't develop with dusting; experts may use cyanoacrylate ("super glue") fuming or laser.
- **Patent (visible) fingerprints**: visible to the eye, deposited via materials like blood, paint, soot, or soil.
- **Fingerprint comparison standards**: many databases exist across law enforcement, military, and government records. A legal court order may be needed if a suspect refuses to be printed. Everyone who plausibly handled an item should be printed for elimination purposes.

### 4. Types of Forgery

A forgery is any writing prepared with intent to deceive or defraud, most commonly a signature.

- **Simulated and traced forgeries**: prepared by carefully copying or tracing a genuine example.
  - Often lacks enough of the forger's own handwriting characteristics for expert identification.
  - **Key detection tip**: no one signs their name exactly the same way twice — if two signatures look identical, at least one is a forgery, likely a tracing. Check by superimposing the signature areas under a bright light.
  - Additional clues: faint indentations on the original that was traced, transferred ink on the reverse side, or latent fingerprints on the source document.
- **Freehand forgeries**: the writer signs another's name without knowing what the real signature looks like. Usually identifiable by experts.
- **Auto-forgeries**: a person's true-name signature, intentionally distorted or disguised so they can later deny it. Common in false travelers-cheque theft claims, disputed loan documents, and credit card fraud.

### 5. Handwriting Analysis

- **Limitations**: a document examiner cannot reliably determine a writer's age, gender, race, physical or mental condition, personality, character, or handedness.
- **Three possible outcomes** of a comparison: non-identification, identification, or inconclusive (often from insufficient samples or working from a photocopy rather than the original).
- **Individual characteristics**: unique to a person (like DNA or fingerprints), shaped by age, physical development, skill acquisition, education, and personal/professional writing habits.
- **Class characteristics**: shared by a group, not unique to an individual; writings can look superficially similar but be genuinely different.
- **Copy-book styles**: shared letter shapes/style from being taught the same original system, sometimes retained into adulthood.
- **Copycat styles**: adopting a respected teacher's, parent's, or peer group's handwriting style (e.g., "bubble writing"), usually abandoned with maturity.
- **Variation**: no one writes identically twice, but each writer has their own natural range — several samples are needed to establish that range. Identical signatures indicate a forgery, likely a tracing.
- **Disguise**: a deliberate effort to change or conceal one's own writing so it can be denied later, usually by changing slant, size, capital-letter shapes, loop shapes, or switching to block printing. Can succeed, especially with limited writing samples.
- **Distortion**: unintentional deviation beyond a writer's natural range, from temporary impairment (illness, injury), substance use, extreme physical weakness, or an unusual writing surface/environment. Can also be permanent from extreme age. Severe distortion can defeat even expert identification.

## Subjective Topics

### 1. Cognitive Interview

- **Effectiveness**: proven more effective and more reliable in court than hypnosis-based techniques.
- **Origin**: developed by Dr. Geiselman at UCLA.
- **Two phases**:
  1. **Narrative phase** — the witness tells the whole story. The investigator guides this by asking the witness to reconstruct the circumstances, report everything (even seemingly trivial details), recall events in different orders, and change perspectives.
  2. **Specific details phase** — tracks down who was involved: physical appearance and clothing, names (working through the alphabet to jar memory on a first letter, then syllables/length), speech characteristics (accent, unusual voice, repeated words, resemblance to someone else), and unusual reactions in the conversation.
- **Benefit**: research shows it improves witness recall compared to standard interview techniques.

### 2. Kinesic Interview

- **Purpose**: not aimed at a confession, but at assessing truthfulness through body language and physical signs of deception.
- **Definition** (Link and Foster): a technique for gaining information from someone not willingly or intentionally disclosing it.
- **Underlying concept**: built entirely on stress — an event or circumstance that forces someone's mind or body out of psychological equilibrium, triggering a subconscious "fight or flight" reaction.
- **Meaningful behaviour**: the signs an interviewer watches for, generally subconscious to the interviewee.
- **Three categories**:
  1. **Self-initiated verbal statements**: unprompted changes in speech pattern (speeding up, hesitation, stammering), excessive politeness, or indirect answers — subconscious attempts to dodge questioning. Dishonest interviewees tend to produce more of these than truthful ones.
  2. **Prompted verbal responses**: reactions to structured questions.
     - *Punishment question* ("What should happen to the criminal?"): an innocent person tends to advocate harsh punishment; a guilty person tends to argue for leniency or understanding.
     - *Physical evidence question* ("Any reason your fingerprints might be near the scene?"): doesn't assert evidence exists. An innocent person answers "no" without concern; a guilty person often shows concern and improvises another lie.
     - *Crime existence question* ("Do you think this crime was even committed?"): a guilty party is more likely to answer "no" (seeing an escape route); an innocent person will likely answer "yes."
  3. **Nonverbal behaviour / body language**: positioning, movement, stillness, and physiological changes, evaluated against the subject's "normal" baseline.
     - Eyes: breaking contact, closing eyes, or lowering the head (though eye-contact norms vary culturally).
     - Eyebrow movement: signals disbelief or concern.
     - Touching the face: nose or chin touching under stress.
     - Blushing: increased blood pressure from stress.
     - Adam's apple movement and exposed neck veins when nervous.
     - Defensive postures: crossed arms or legs.

### 3. Reid Technique

- **Description**: an interrogation method that creates a high-pressure environment followed by sympathy and offers of help, conditional on a confession. Presented as a monologue rather than a question-and-answer exchange.
- **Three-phase process**: fact analysis, a non-accusatory behavior analysis interview, then the nine steps of interrogation if the evidence points to involvement.
- **The nine steps**:
  1. **Positive confrontation**: tell the suspect the evidence points to them; offer an early chance to explain.
  2. **Shift the blame**: develop themes that psychologically justify or excuse the crime, shifting blame to circumstances or others.
  3. **Minimize denials**: reduce the frequency of the suspect's denials.
  4. **Address excuses**: turn offered excuses into a path toward acknowledgment.
  5. **Reinforce sincerity**: keep the suspect receptive.
  6. **Theme discussion toward alternatives**: the suspect quiets and listens; crying at this point is read as an indicator of guilt.
  7. **Pose the "alternative question"**: offer two explanations, one more socially acceptable than the other — either choice implies guilt (a third, unprompted option to deny everything always technically exists).
  8. **Repeat admission**: have the suspect repeat the admission in front of witnesses and gather corroborating detail.
  9. **Document admission**: record the confession (audio, video, or written).
- **Criticism**: critics argue the method's "guilt-presumptive" framing can push innocent suspects toward false confession through misclassification (mistaking truthfulness for deception), coercion (psychological pressure), and contamination (police inadvertently feeding non-public details that end up in the "confession").

### 4. Document Analysis

- **Role of documents**: they can help or hurt a case depending on how they're presented and preserved; relevance isn't always obvious early, so err toward including rather than excluding.
- **Documents vs. witnesses**: documents don't make cases, witnesses do — documents make or break a witness's credibility, and paper-heavy cases can confuse or bore a jury.
- **General rules for document collection**:
  - Obtain originals where feasible; work from copies and keep originals segregated.
  - Handle originals as little as possible, since they may need forensic analysis later.
  - Maintain a good filing system (sequential stamping helps) since a lost key document can damage the case.
- **Obtaining documentary evidence**: forged, altered, and fabricated documents are common in fraud cases. Contracts, invoices, wills, and statements can establish that fraud occurred, its scope, and who's responsible. A fraud examiner isn't expected to be a document expert but should know how to spot likely-phony documents and when to bring in one.
- **Value of expert forensic examination**: helps prove the fraud theory, corroborate or refute witness/suspect statements, provide leverage in interviews, and support positive identification of a writer (useful for proving intent). Experts may testify at trial. Early consultation, not just pre-trial, helps narrow investigative scope.
- **Common examination types**: forged signatures, identifying writers/hand printers, detecting alterations and restoring erasures, determining when a document was prepared, detecting counterfeits, and restoring faint indented writing.
- **Handling documents as physical evidence**:
  - Store in a sealed, initialed, dated folder or envelope.
  - Use originals for forensic exams; photocopies often lack sufficient clarity.
  - Mark evidence with date and initials in a noncritical area, using a different instrument than the one used on the document — or mark the container instead if marking the document risks indentation.
  - Don't fold, staple, clip, or crumple; store photocopies in paper folders rather than plastic sleeves (which can stick and damage features).
  - **Chain of custody**: a record of who possessed an item and what they did with it, needed for court acceptance and to establish authenticity.
- **Spotting phony documents without an expert**:
  - Signature forgeries: compare side-by-side for irregularities or size differences (accounting for age, health, injury, or substance effects).
  - Substituted pages: hold pages to bright light to compare whiteness, density, thickness, opacity, and fiber pattern.
  - Ink differences/alterations/erasures: check under bright or angled light for color, reflectivity, or surface disturbance.
  - Counterfeit printed documents: compare side-by-side with genuine versions; watch for outdated form revisions.
  - Suspicious indented writing: reduce room light and shine a beam low and parallel to the page.
