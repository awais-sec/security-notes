# Fraud Examination Fundamentals

Consolidated notes on white-collar crime and fraud examination, merged from two overlapping source documents with duplicate sections combined.

## 1. White-Collar Crime and Fraud

**Definition**: "A crime committed by a person of respectability and high social status in the course of their occupation."

- Nonviolent, financially motivated crimes.
- Commonly committed by individuals, corporations, and government professionals.
- FBI perspective: "Lying, cheating, and stealing — that's white-collar crime in a nutshell."

**Impact**: not victimless. A single scam can wreck companies, ruin families, and cost billions. Global cost is estimated around $4.5 trillion USD, with an average loss of roughly 5% of revenue per victim organization.

## 2. Why People Commit Fraud: The Fraud Triangle

1. **Perceived pressure**: financial problems the person feels they can't share — greed, living beyond their means, high bills or poor credit, unexpected expenses.
2. **Perceived opportunity**: conditions that allow fraud — weak internal controls, poor oversight, lack of audits, ignorance or apathy.
3. **Rationalization**: how fraudsters justify it to themselves — "I'll pay it back," "nobody is getting hurt," "I deserve more."

**Statistics**: roughly 80% of people may commit fraud if the opportunity is present, about 10% will always commit fraud, and about 10% will never commit fraud.

## 3. Profile of a White-Collar Criminal

- Typically a trusted insider (manager, financial employee).
- Behavior often seems out of character once discovered.
- Common traits reported: friendly (39%), highly respected (35%), extroverted (33%), intellectual (22%).

## 4. Red Flags

A red flag is a circumstance that's unusual or deviates from normal activity — a signal that something might be amiss and warrants further investigation. Red flags don't indicate guilt or innocence on their own; they're warning signs.

**Management red flags**

- Reluctance to provide information to auditors
- Managers frequently disputing with auditors
- Decision-making dominated by a single person or small group
- Disrespect for regulatory bodies
- Weak internal control environment
- Inexperienced or negligent accounting personnel
- Decentralization without oversight
- Excessive number of checking accounts
- Frequent changes in banking or external audit services
- Selling company assets below market value
- Downsizing in a healthy market
- Recurrent loan rollovers

**Employee red flags**

- Lavish lifestyle changes: expensive cars, jewelry, or homes
- Significant personal debt or credit issues
- Behavioral changes (drugs, alcohol, gambling, job fear)
- High turnover in sensitive roles
- Refusal to take vacations or sick leave
- Lack of duty segregation in vulnerable areas

## 5. Fighting Fraud

**Comprehensive strategy**: prevention, proactive detection, investigation, and legal follow-up.

**Fraud prevention tactics**:

- Ethical leadership ("tone at the top")
- Employee education and awareness
- Strong controls and regular audits
- Anonymous reporting systems
- Internal and external audits
- Monitoring and risk assessment

## 6. Famous Corporate Scandals

- **Enron (2001)**: $74 billion loss to shareholders; massive debt concealed; whistleblower Sherron Watkins.
- **WorldCom (2002)**: $11 billion in fake assets; 30,000 jobs lost; uncovered by internal audit.
- **Tyco (2002)**: CEO and CFO stole $150 million; revenue inflated by $500 million.

## 7. Fraud Examination vs. Forensic Accounting

**Fraud examination**: the systematic process of investigating fraud from start to finish. Key components are obtaining evidence, reporting, testifying, and preventing future fraud.

- **Reporting**: clear, concise, unbiased — oral or written, shared with decision-makers.
- **Testifying**: accurate, professional presentation of findings in legal proceedings.
- **Prevention role**: fraud examiners recommend policies to reduce future risk.

**Forensic accounting**: in-depth analysis involving financial records, legal matters, and cyber fraud detection.

| Aspect | Fraud Examination | Forensic Accounting |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Fraud resolution | Legal/accounting investigation |
| Practitioner | Anyone with investigation skills | Typically trained accountants |
| Litigation role | Always assumes litigation | May or may not involve legal action |

## 8. Fraud Examination Methodology

Fraud examination moves from general to specific, focusing evidence analysis toward the suspected perpetrator, based on **predication** — a reasonable basis to believe fraud has occurred. Investigating without predication risks legal exposure.

**Key principles**:

1. **Assume litigation will follow**: collect and preserve evidence within legal frameworks from the start.
2. **Act on predication**: only investigate when there's a reasonable basis to believe fraud occurred.
3. **Approach from two perspectives simultaneously**: seek to prove fraud occurred, and seek to disprove it. This dual approach keeps the examination objective and legally sound.
4. **Move from general to specific**:
   - Interview neutral witnesses first (least to most informed).
   - Then speak with complicit parties (least to most culpable).
   - End with the primary suspect.
5. **Use the Fraud Theory Approach**, in four steps:
   - Analyze available data.
   - Create a hypothesis.
   - Test the hypothesis.
   - Refine and amend as needed (using a "worst case" scenario to start, then revising as facts emerge).

## 9. Fraud Response Plan

A fraud response plan defines how an organization reacts once fraud is suspected. It reduces risk, controls damage, and signals that fraud is taken seriously — the goal is a fast, consistent response that minimizes damage and demonstrates zero tolerance.

**Key components**:

- **Reporting protocols**: who gets alerted immediately.
- **Response team**: legal, HR, audit, finance, and IT.
- **Decision-making criteria**: objective factors like credibility, seriousness, or prior incidents.
- **Litigation hold procedures**: pause document destruction processes.
- **Documentation principles**: record every phase and decision.
- **Fraud incident report log**: track every allegation and action taken.

**Initial response to allegations**:

1. Activate the response team.
2. Consult legal counsel.
3. Notify the insurer.
4. Preserve evidence (lock down documents and systems).
5. Limit access.
6. Conduct a preliminary review.

**Initial assessment**: a quick review to decide whether a full investigation is warranted — interview key individuals, review documents, and log every decision.

## 10. Planning and Conducting a Formal Investigation

1. **Engagement letters**: short form (flexible, broad scope) or long form (specific techniques outlined).
2. **Assembling the team**: roles may include Certified Fraud Examiners, legal counsel, HR, forensic accountants, IT specialists, and external consultants.
3. **Understand the organization**: its industry, operations, structure, and risks.
4. **Build an investigation plan**: define scope, roles, and timelines; protect confidentiality; consider legal implications; adapt resources as needed.

## 11. Applied Case Studies

### Case A: Digital Fraud and Suspected Embezzlement

**Preserving digital evidence**:

- Issue a litigation hold with legal support.
- Suspend document deletion policies.
- Lock down access to sensitive digital files.
- Involve IT; use encryption and passwords.
- Avoid tipping off suspects — operate discreetly.

**Applying the Fraud Theory Approach**:

1. Analyze data: transaction logs, complaints.
2. Create hypothesis: fictitious vendors used to siphon funds.
3. Test hypothesis: investigate vendor records and payment anomalies.
4. Revise hypothesis: adjust if kickbacks or other schemes surface.

**Preventing tipping off**:

- Know who's being investigated and what they can access.
- Limit internal disclosures.
- Work off-hours if needed.
- Follow confidentiality clauses and IT policies.

**Interview order**:

1. Neutral witnesses (least to most knowledgeable)
2. Complicit parties (least to most culpable)
3. Primary suspect (last, once the facts are in hand)

**Applying fraud response protocols**: activate the response team, seek legal guidance, notify the insurer, lock down documents and systems, and document every action in a report log.

### Case B: Suspicious Vendor Payments and Lifestyle Clues

**Working hypothesis**: the purchasing officer is either receiving kickbacks for inflated invoices, or has created a fictitious vendor for personal gain.

**Evidence needed**:

- Vendor payments over the past 18 months
- Invoice legitimacy verification
- Supplier pricing compared against industry norms
- The officer's financials and bank records
- Vendor onboarding documentation
- Relationships and shared assets

**Interview sequence**:

1. Internal auditors
2. Accounts payable staff
3. Other purchasing employees
4. Vendor representatives
5. Purchasing officer

**Key red flags**: sudden lifestyle upgrades, weak procurement controls, opaque performance metrics, no history of consequences for prior fraud, inaccessible financial data, and irregular vendor activity.

**Maintaining confidentiality**:

- Lock up physical records.
- Use password protection and firewalls.
- Mark materials "Confidential."
- Avoid public or electronic discussion of the case.
- Limit who knows the investigation is happening.
- Engage legal counsel to extend privilege protection.
